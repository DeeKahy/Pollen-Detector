\section{Imaging tests}\label{sec:imaging-tests}

Before building more hardware around the camera, we wanted to know what it can actually see.
We had two questions. How big do things look in the picture, so that we can measure distances
and sizes? And what do colours look like, since pollen is mostly yellowish brown?

The camera is the Raspberry Pi HQ camera (Sony IMX477, 4056~$\times$~3040 pixels) with the lens
removed. The tape lies on top of the lens mount, and the light shines through the tape onto the
sensor. There is no lens, so the picture is a shadow of what is on the tape. One pixel is
1.55~$\mu$m wide, so a birch pollen grain of about 22~$\mu$m is about 14 pixels across.
During all the tests the IR-cut filter was still in place in the lens mount. Because of the
filter and the walls of the mount, there was an air gap of about 11.5~mm between the tape and
the sensor.

As a test object we put a light brown hair on the tape. A hair is 50 to 100~$\mu$m wide, so it
should be clearly visible, and it is easy to recognise. The first results were more promising
than we expected, given that we only used normal room light and a cheap blue LED. The hair was
clearly visible in most of the pictures taken with room light.

\subsection{Test 1: room light and a blue LED}\label{sec:imaging-test-1}

\paragraph{Method.}
We changed one camera setting at a time and took a picture of the same spot on the tape after each
change. We did this twice:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Series 1:} ceiling light on, LED pointed away from the tape. All light comes from the room.
  \item \textbf{Series 2:} room dark, blue LED pointed at the tape. The LED is the only real light source.
\end{itemize}

In series~2 the LED was about 30~cm above the tape.
Both series use the same ten settings, so every picture in series~1 has a partner in series~2.
Table~\ref{tab:camtest-settings} lists them. Every picture was saved together with a note and all
camera settings in a JSON file, using the web interface described in Section~\ref{sec:webui-3}.
This makes it possible to go back later and see exactly how each picture was taken.

\paragraph{Light levels.}
With automatic exposure the camera chose 5195~$\mu$s at gain~1 under room light (\#06), and
9976~$\mu$s at gain~1.9 with only the blue LED (\#16). So the LED gives about three to four times
less light than the room. \#23 used that brightness with exposure time only (19\,000~$\mu$s,
gain~1), but without automatic white balance the whole picture was clipped to pure blue and
showed nothing.

\paragraph{Distances and sizes.}
Without a lens, the size of an object in the picture is close to its real size. Light from the
LED spreads out a little between the tape and the sensor, which makes the shadow slightly larger
than the object. With the LED at a distance $z_1$ above the tape and a gap $z_2$ between the tape
and the sensor, the magnification is
\begin{equation}
  M = \frac{z_1 + z_2}{z_1}.
\end{equation}
In our setup the gap is $z_2 \approx 11.5$~mm, and in all the LED pictures the LED was about
30~cm above the tape. With $z_1 \approx 30$~cm,
$M \approx 1.04$, so one pixel shows about 1.5~$\mu$m of tape instead of 1.55~$\mu$m.
The light source is not a point, though. A source of width $s$ makes every edge blurry by about
$s \cdot z_2 / z_1$. For a 3~mm LED at 30~cm and our gap this is about 0.1~mm, which is several
pollen grains wide.
This is why the hair looks sharp in the whole picture but is only a soft band when we zoom in
to full resolution (Figure~\ref{fig:camtest-blur}). The gap is the biggest problem: with the
filter removed and the tape closer to the sensor, the blur gets much smaller.

\paragraph{Results.}
The figures show the whole picture, about 6.3~$\times$~4.7~mm of tape.
With room light the hair was clearly visible in almost every picture, even when the picture was
too dark (\#04) or too bright (\#05). The best was the greyscale picture (\#09). Most room-light
pictures also had a strong pink cast, which greyscale removes.
With the blue LED only a few pictures were even remotely good. The best was the one with double
contrast (\#21). Figure~\ref{fig:camtest-best} shows the best picture of each series.

\paragraph{Problem found during the tests.}
While testing we found that a picture could show an old frame, taken before the last settings
change or before the LED was switched on. The camera only kept one frame in memory, and that frame
was returned instead of a new one. We fixed this in the device software: it now waits for a new frame
and skips frames until the exposure matches the setting. All pictures in this section were taken
after the fix.

\paragraph{What we learned.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Without a lens the camera clearly shows a hair (50 to 100~$\mu$m) in the whole picture.
  At full resolution everything is blurred, mainly because of the 11.5~mm gap between tape and sensor.
  \fillin{TODO: smallest thing you could see, in $\mu$m}
  \item Room light gave better pictures than the blue LED. The best picture was the greyscale
  picture with room light (\#09). Greyscale looked at least as good as colour.
  \item With the blue LED only a few pictures were usable. The best was the one with double
  contrast (\#21).
  \item We think the problem is the colour of the LED, not its brightness. With pure blue light only
  one in four pixels on the sensor sees anything, because of the colour filter on the sensor.
  A lighter blue, with more white mixed in, should work better.
  \item The blue LED gives enough light, but needs an exposure of around 20~ms at gain~1.
  \item Gain makes the picture noisier \fillin{TODO: confirm from \#07/\#17}; use a longer exposure instead.
\end{itemize}
